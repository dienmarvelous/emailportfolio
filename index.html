What actually is a blockchain?
Welcome to your first lesson. Before we talk about Bitcoin prices or NFTs or yield farming — we need to understand the technology underneath it all. And the good news? It's not nearly as complicated as crypto Twitter makes it sound.
The one-sentence version: A blockchain is a shared record book that thousands of computers around the world all hold a copy of — and none of them can secretly edit it without everyone else noticing.
Think of your bank. Right now, your balance exists as a number in your bank's private database. You trust them not to change it. With a blockchain, there is no central database. Instead, every transaction is bundled into a "block," stamped with a timestamp and a unique fingerprint, then chained to the block before it.
Change one block? Every block after it immediately breaks. The network catches it instantly. That's what makes it tamper-resistant — not magic, just math.
BLOCK
A container of recent transactions, a timestamp, and the fingerprint of the previous block.
CHAIN
Each block references the one before it, creating an unbroken, verifiable history.
NODE
A computer that holds a full copy of the blockchain and validates new transactions.
CONSENSUS
The rule system nodes use to agree on which transactions are valid.
You don't need to memorize any of this yet. Just hold onto the core idea: a blockchain replaces "trust in one institution" with "trust in transparent, distributed math."
Next lesson, we'll get hands-on — we're building your first wallet and explaining the difference between a public key and a private key (and why you must never share the second one).

Your keys, your crypto.
Lose the keys, lose everything.
Last lesson, you learned what a blockchain is — a distributed ledger no single party controls. Today's lesson is the most important thing we'll teach you this entire course. We're serious.
The rule: "Not your keys, not your coins." In crypto, whoever holds the private key controls the funds. There is no "forgot my password" button. No customer support line. No bank to call.
Here's how the system works: every crypto wallet generates two keys — a public key and a private key. Think of your public key like your email address — you share it freely so people can send you funds. Your private key is the password that proves ownership and authorizes transactions.
But here's the twist: your private key isn't stored on the blockchain. It never leaves your device (or your head, if you memorize your seed phrase). That's what self-custody means — you are the bank.
PUBLIC KEY
Your wallet address. Share it freely. Used to receive funds.
PRIVATE KEY
Your proof of ownership. Never share it. Controls your funds.
SEED PHRASE
12–24 words that back up your private key. Store offline, physically.
HOT vs COLD
Hot wallets are internet-connected. Cold wallets are offline. Cold = safer for large amounts.
The most common beginner mistake? Keeping funds on an exchange long-term. Exchanges hold your keys on your behalf — meaning they technically hold your crypto. Not you. Move meaningful amounts to a self-custodied wallet once you're comfortable.
In our final lesson, we're stepping into DeFi — decentralized finance. You'll see exactly why blockchains and wallets make entirely new financial instruments possible.

DeFi: banking, rebuilt from scratch.
You've made it to the final lesson. You understand the ledger. You control your keys. Now let's see what's actually possible when you combine those two things.
DeFi in one line: Decentralized Finance = financial services (lending, trading, earning interest) that run on smart contracts instead of banks and brokers.
A smart contract is just code that lives on a blockchain and executes automatically when certain conditions are met. No middleman. No office hours. No loan officers deciding your credit score. If the condition is true, the contract runs.
In practice, DeFi protocols let you lend your crypto to borrowers and earn interest automatically; trade tokens peer-to-peer without a centralized exchange; or provide liquidity to pools and collect a share of trading fees — all 24/7, globally, from your wallet.
SMART CONTRACT
Self-executing code on a blockchain. Runs automatically when conditions are met.
DEX
Decentralized Exchange. Trade tokens directly from your wallet, no sign-up needed.
LIQUIDITY POOL
A shared pool of funds that powers trading. Providers earn a cut of transaction fees.
YIELD
Returns earned by supplying assets to DeFi protocols. Rates fluctuate with demand.
DeFi is powerful — and risky. Smart contracts can have bugs. Protocols can be exploited. Always research before connecting your wallet to any protocol. Start small. Understand what you're doing before you do it.
That's the Fundamentals Track complete. You now understand more about how crypto actually works than 90% of people who own it. The next step is the Intermediate Track — we go protocol-deep on Ethereum, Solana, and Layer 2s.

Maison Velours
Dressed for the life you actually live
A note from our founder
I burned out of fashion.
Then I made it again.

I spent ten years as a buyer for a major European retailer. I flew to eight cities a season. I told factories to move faster. I approved fabrics I knew wouldn't last. And then one day I was sitting in a sampling room in Guangzhou, holding a blouse that would retail for €280 and cost €7 to produce, and I thought: this isn't fashion. This is just product.

I left. I spent two years doing nothing but visiting mills and learning to understand cloth — really understand it. How it drapes when it breathes. How it moves differently after the third wash. I was looking for fabrics that earn the right to be worn.

Maison Velours launched with nine pieces. We sold out in six weeks with zero advertising — just women who found something that felt, for once, like it was made for them. We've grown slowly since. That's intentional.

The name comes from the French word for velvet — not the fabric necessarily, but the quality. Something that resists, slightly, and rewards the touch.

"I wanted clothes you could live in.
Not just photograph in."

Welcome to the family

grandmother is watching.
Because she is. (Hi, Mama Ade. We know you're reading this.)

Here's the honest story: Petit Four Bakehouse started in a home kitchen in Lekki, with a single recipe for a croissant that our founder, Adaeze, had spent three years trying to perfect.

Not because she went to culinary school. Not because she had a business plan. Because she had eaten one extraordinary croissant in Lyon in 2014 — buttery, audibly crispy on the outside, almost custardy in the middle — and had never found it again. So she made it herself. Again and again and again.

The croissant that started everything

Laminated dough. 81 layers of butter. 72 hours of cold proofing. Three folds over two days. Baked to order in small batches of twelve. The recipe has never changed. The process has never been rushed. To this day, it's the most ordered item on our menu.

Word spread the way word still spreads in Lagos: someone tasted it, texted a friend, and suddenly Adaeze was baking 200 croissants a week out of her apartment. In 2017, we opened the Bakehouse.

We now ship nationwide. But every single pastry is still made the slow way, by the same small team, using the same obsessive standards. Because we believe the way food is made is part of how it tastes.
